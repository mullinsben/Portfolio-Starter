The Em-Dash Manifesto

It’s not a hyphen—and it’s certainly not an en-dash. It’s the em-dash: the longest, loudest, most dramatically gifted mark in the whole drawer. This is placeholder copy for the Portfolio Starter template—replace it with something true.

By Your Name January 1, 2026 4 min read

Why the em-dash

Let me be clear—and I will, because the em-dash makes clarity feel theatrical. Most punctuation does a job and gets out of the way. The em-dash does a job and takes a bow. It’s not a workhorse; it’s a showhorse—and every sentence it touches stands up a little straighter.

It’s not a pause. It’s a held breath.

This whole page is Em-Dash Ipsum—placeholder text that exists to show you the rhythm of a paragraph before you have anything to put in it. Read it once for the shape, then delete every word and write your own. The template won’t mind. It was built for exactly this.

It’s not a hyphen

The hyphen is small, polite, and load-bearing—it glues well-meaning to compound and goes home. Useful! Humble! But it is not the em-dash, and it would never claim to be. Confusing the two is like confusing a comma with a trumpet solo.

This is also where the template shows off its floated figure pattern—a tall image that text wraps around. Swap the capybara for a screenshot, a diagram, or a photo of your actual work. It’s not decoration; it’s pacing.

It’s not an en-dash either

The en-dash is the em-dash’s shorter, more reasonable sibling—the one who shows up for ranges (pages 10–12) and scores (a 3–1 win) and never makes a scene. Respectable. Measured. Boring. The em-dash, by contrast, is the sibling who interrupts dinner with a monologue—and somehow everyone’s glad they did.

“It’s not punctuation—it’s a personality.” — this template, about itself

The held breath

Here’s the real argument. A period stops you. A comma slows you. But an em-dash suspends you—it holds the door open just long enough for the next idea to walk through and surprise you. That’s the trick the best writers steal: set up an expectation, drop the dash, then land somewhere better than the reader guessed.

Notice what just happened—a section heading, a paragraph, a pull-quote, a refrain, a floated image, and a full-width figure, all in one essay. That’s the entire toolkit. Mix them however your story needs.

How to abuse it responsibly

One em-dash per sentence is seasoning. Three is a cry for help. The mark is so charismatic that it’s easy to lean on it—which is, admittedly, the entire joke of this template. Use it when the sentence earns a beat. Skip it when a comma will do. And if a robot ever tells you it’s “not just X—it’s Y,” well—you know who taught it that.
